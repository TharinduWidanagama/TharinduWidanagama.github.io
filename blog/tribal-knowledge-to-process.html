Turning tribal knowledge into a process anyone can follow

5 min read · Published 21 August 2026

Every team has one. The process that only really works because one specific person remembers the three exceptions, the one client who always does it differently, and the workaround from two systems ago. It isn't written down because, until it breaks, nobody notices it needs to be.

Start by shadowing, not interviewing

Asking someone to describe their process from memory produces a cleaner, more logical version than what they actually do — people tidy up their own workflow when narrating it. Watching them do the task live surfaces the exceptions and shortcuts that never make it into a description.

Write the exceptions down first

The main path of most processes is intuitive enough that people can figure it out. It's the edge cases — the cancelled order, the client on a legacy plan, the request that arrives on a Friday afternoon — that cause the most support tickets and the most reliance on one tenured employee.

In practice: a one-page "if this happens, do this" exceptions sheet often prevents more confusion than a full step-by-step manual.

Test the document on someone who's never done the task

A process document reviewed only by the person who wrote it will always look complete to them, because they're filling gaps with knowledge they don't realise they're using. Hand it to a new hire, watch where they get stuck, and revise from there.

Keep ownership even after it's documented

Documentation goes stale the moment nobody's responsible for updating it. Assign a clear owner for each process document, and treat "this doc is now out of date" as a normal, expected piece of feedback rather than a failure.

  • Shadow the task before you document it
  • Document the exceptions before the happy path
  • Test the document on someone unfamiliar with the task
  • Assign an owner so it doesn't go stale
